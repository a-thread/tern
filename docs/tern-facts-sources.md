# Tern facts: sources

Citations for `src/today/components/WeekStrip/utils/ternFacts.ts` (252 facts). Each fact is listed under the source that supports it. The final section lists facts with no recorded source.

Egevang 2010 and Fijn 2013 are the primary papers. Henri et al. 2020 is a peer-reviewed interview study. BirdLife and MassWildlife are institutional pages. Wikipedia, the National Wildlife Federation and National Geographic are secondary sources. The tour operators’ pages (Secret Atlas, Oceanwide, Polar Latitudes, Natural Habitat) are the weakest.

## 1. Egevang et al. 2010, PNAS

Egevang C, Stenhouse IJ, Phillips RA, Petersen A, Fox JW, Silk JRD. 2010. Tracking of Arctic terns Sterna paradisaea reveals longest animal migration. PNAS 107:2078–2081. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0909493107

Eleven terns tracked from Greenland (10) and Iceland (1) in 2007–08. Read in full (most of the text).

- An Arctic tern doesn’t fly nonstop. It stops along the way to feed and rest, and it reaches the far end all the same.
- A geolocator records light levels. The times of sunrise and sunset tell scientists roughly where a bird has been.
- In one year an Arctic tern flies farther than the distance around the Earth at the equator.
- Scientists catch terns gently at the nest to fit a tag, and later catch them again to collect it.
- Arctic terns may move to a different breeding site after predators raid their colony or fish run short.
- More than half of the world’s Arctic terns breed in Greenland and Iceland.
- The first big review of where Arctic terns migrate, in 1967, was pieced together from ring recoveries and sightings.
- Arctic terns breed across the far north, from Greenland and Iceland to Alaska and Siberia.
- An Arctic tern flies about 44,000 miles (70,000 km) every year, the longest migration of any animal.
- A long-lived Arctic tern may fly over 1.5 million miles in its lifetime, about three trips to the Moon and back.
- Terns don’t fly straight south. They follow a winding, S-shaped route down the Atlantic, using the winds to make the trip easier.
- Nobody knew how far Arctic terns really travel until 2010, when tiny trackers small enough for a bird this light traced whole journeys.
- Heading home from Antarctica to south Greenland, tracked terns covered about 24,000 km in only 40 days, roughly 520 km a day.
- At about 38 to 40°S, tracked terns stopped heading south and drifted east and west, and three of them wandered into the Indian Ocean, one as far as 106°E.
- Tracked terns flew about 34,600 km south in 93 days but only about 25,700 km north in 40, a shorter trip made in less than half the time.
- Migrating Arctic terns usually travel in small flocks of fewer than 15 birds, yet the tracked birds crossed the equator within days of each other.
- For decades an Arctic tern’s round trip was quoted as 40,000 km, until tracking showed that birds were flying nearly twice as far.
- Trackers fitted to Greenland and Icelandic terns in 2007 showed them averaging about 70,000 km a year, far more than anyone had guessed.
- Tracked terns kept moving around their Antarctic feeding grounds, covering about 11,000 km on average there.
- The tracked terns spent the southern summer south of 58°S, along the edge of the Antarctic ice.
- After breeding, Arctic terns from Greenland and Iceland stop for about three weeks in the North Atlantic, then split toward the coasts of Africa or Brazil.
- Those trackers were geolocators, loggers so small they weighed about a gram and a half, lighter than a sugar cube.
- The tracked terns’ North Atlantic stopover lies where cold, productive northern water meets warmer southern water, a spot rich in chlorophyll.
- Tracked Greenland terns reached the North Atlantic stopover around 22 August on average and left it around 15 September.
- Tracked Greenland terns reached their Antarctic winter waters around 24 November and left in mid-April, spending about 149 days there.
- The tracked terns’ return trip curved counterclockwise around the South Atlantic and clockwise around the North Atlantic, following the ocean’s wind systems.
- Tracked terns’ routes overlapped those of Manx and Cory’s shearwaters, showing that small size is no handicap when riding the Atlantic’s winds.
- On the way home, the tracked terns stayed over deep water, far from the edges of the continental shelves.
- Greenland terns recaptured after their year-long journey weighed about the same as before, around 106 to 110 grams.
- The tracked terns spent the austral summer in the Weddell Sea region, which holds more Antarctic krill than other parts of the Southern Ocean.
- A tern tagged in Iceland followed much the same route as the terns tagged in Greenland, even though it set off earlier.
- The 2007 tracking study fitted terns at Sand Island in northeast Greenland (74°N) and Flatey Island in Iceland (65°N).
- In 2007, 50 Greenland terns and 20 Icelandic terns were fitted with loggers, and 11 loggers were recovered the following year.
- The zigzag route is longer on the map than a straight line, but it follows favorable winds, so it is easier in the air.
- On the way north, many Arctic terns take a different path from the one they flew south.
- On the way south, terns often pause in the middle of the North Atlantic for a few weeks to feed before the long run down.
- Scientists were surprised to find that tracked terns moved around a lot in the south instead of staying in one spot.
- Tracking showed that some terns linger for weeks at a favorite feeding spot before moving on.

## 2. Morten et al. 2023, Global Change Biology

Morten JM, Buchanan PJ, Egevang C, et al. 2023. Global warming and Arctic terns: estimating climate change impacts on the world’s longest migration. Global Change Biology 29(19):5596–5614. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10946559/

Read the first 100,000 characters. It gives a lifespan of up to 34 years with an average adult lifespan of 11–19 years, a northbound passage of 25–46 days, and a pack-ice wintering range centered on East Antarctica and the Weddell, Ross and Amundsen seas.

- Arctic terns can live up to 34 years, though the average adult lifespan is about 11 to 19 years.
- In the Antarctic, Arctic terns concentrate in the pack-ice zone, notably off East Antarctica and in the Weddell, Ross and Amundsen seas.
- Arctic terns recorded on the way north have taken between 25 and 46 days to fly from Antarctic waters back to the Arctic.

## 3. Fijn et al. 2013, Ardea

- A Dutch tern’s breeding colony and its farthest winter spot lie almost exactly on opposite sides of the globe.

- After breeding, Dutch terns first stay at sea near the North Sea, then head west through the English Channel or the Irish Sea.
- Dutch terns reached Antarctic waters around 12 November, after about 110 days and 29,700 km of travel.
- In Antarctica, tracked Dutch terns drifted slowly westward along the ice edge between mid-November and mid-March.
- Arctic terns are among the most abundant birds off East Antarctica in the southern summer, according to ship surveys.
- Unlike some Greenland birds, none of the tracked Dutch terns followed the South American coast. They took a more direct route across the Atlantic.
- Researchers suggest that terns from Norway, Svalbard or Arctic Russia could migrate even farther than Dutch terns if they winter off East Antarctica.
- Greenland terns lingered about a month at their North Atlantic stopover, while Dutch terns stayed only about a week.
- Two tracked Dutch terns spent time in the Great Australian Bight on their way south to Antarctica.
- Dutch terns spent most of the austral summer in the Southern Ocean between about 35°E and 150°E.
- Researchers once suggested that terns circle Antarctica before flying north, but tracking of Dutch terns found no sign of it.
- A ringed Danish tern was recovered in Wilkes Land, East Antarctica, long before trackers confirmed that terns winter there.

- Peregrine falcons and merlins, small falcons, can prey on Arctic terns, chicks especially, according to Inuit harvesters near Kuujjuaraapik.
